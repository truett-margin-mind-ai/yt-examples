## Part 1: Executive Slide Presentation

---

### Q1: "Whenever you're ready, you can begin your 90-second walkthrough."

**Mock score: 9/10**

**Key rubric points to hit:**
- Open by restating the business question (renewal decision, not a usage report)
- Structure: Usage → Value → Recommendation
- Contextualize every number (don't just list stats)
- Speak decisively, end with a clear recommendation
- Finish in 75-90 seconds

**Answer:**

> "Before I walk through the metrics — the core question we're answering today is whether this investment is delivering enough engagement and employee value to justify renewal. I'll cover three things: utilization depth, employee value indicators, and then my recommendation.
>
> Starting with adoption. 298 employees are actively coaching — that's 60% of your licenses, six months in. At this stage, that's strong early adoption, especially with 12% month-over-month growth continuing. And these aren't one-and-done sessions. Users are averaging 4.2 sessions each — that tells us people are coming back because they're getting value. On current momentum alone, without deploying any new engagement strategies, we're projecting roughly 75% utilization by renewal.
>
> Now — is it working? Emphatically yes. NPS is +45, which puts this program in the top quartile against an industry average of around +30. Overall satisfaction is 4.3 out of 5, and the highest-rated dimension is 'my coach helps me achieve my goals' at 4.5. What I also want to highlight is the survey response rate — 52%, well above the typical 25 to 35%. When more than half your active users take the time to respond, it tells you they care about this program.
>
> So — should we renew? I recommend yes. The foundation is strong — high satisfaction, deep engagement, and sustained adoption growth. A targeted activation strategy, starting now, can strengthen that growth trend and further maximize ROI heading into renewal. There's real upside here.
>
> That's the high-level story — happy to dive deeper into any area."

### Q2 [FOCUS]: "If I don't renew, what do we lose?"

**Mock score: FAIL — answer was too employee-centered, not business-centered.**

**What went wrong:** Focused on individual employee outcomes (reaching goals, feelings) without translating to organizational impact. No reference to adoption momentum, productivity at scale, talent retention, or investment trade-offs.

**Key rubric points to hit:**
- Translate employee outcomes into business outcomes
- Reference current momentum and what gets lost
- Frame as investment trade-off / opportunity cost
- Mention talent retention, leadership development, cultural consistency
- Ground in the specific data (adoption level, engagement depth)

**Answer:**

> "You lose three things — and none of them are easy to rebuild.
>
> First, you lose the momentum. 298 employees are actively coaching, averaging 4.2 sessions each, with 12% month-over-month growth. That engagement wasn't automatic — it took six months of onboarding, matching, and trust-building between employees and coaches. If we stop now, that behavioral change resets. You can't pause a coaching program and pick up where you left off — the relationships dissolve, the habits break, and restarting means rebuilding from zero with a skeptical audience.
>
> Second, you lose a retention and development lever. Employees who are coaching are working on leadership skills, career growth, and performance goals. At a 5,000-person tech company competing for talent, that's not a soft benefit — it's a retention tool. The NPS of +45 and the 4.5 on 'helps me achieve my goals' tell you people see this as genuinely valuable to their careers. Pulling it signals the organization isn't investing in their growth.
>
> Third, there's the sunk cost and opportunity cost. You've paid for 500 licenses for six months. 60% are active and growing. Stopping now means you paid for the ramp but not the return. The ROI compounds in year two when adoption is higher and per-user cost drops. Walking away now is paying for the foundation and then not building the house.
>
> The renewal isn't just 'do we keep coaching.' It's 'do we capitalize on six months of investment, or write it off.'"

### Q5 [FOCUS]: "Why, and how much, should I trust the NPS +45 as a meaningful signal?"

**Mock score: Needs improvement — treated NPS as proof rather than directional signal.**

**What to fix:** Acknowledge possible bias (survey self-selection), explain NPS as directional (not ROI), contextualize with response rate as credibility check, and pair it with engagement data.

**Key rubric points to hit:**
- Acknowledge possible bias openly
- Explain what NPS does and does not measure
- Contextualize +45 with benchmarks
- Reference survey response rate as credibility signal
- Avoid treating it as absolute proof
- Connect to engagement depth as corroboration

**Answer:**

> "NPS +45 is a strong signal, but I want to be honest about what it is and isn't.
>
> What it is: a directional indicator of employee sentiment. +45 puts this program well above the industry benchmark of around +30 for employee development programs. It tells us that significantly more users would recommend this program than detract from it. That's meaningful.
>
> What it isn't: a standalone measure of ROI. NPS measures sentiment, not performance outcomes. It doesn't tell us whether coaching is improving productivity, retention, or leadership capability directly. So I treat it as one data point in a larger picture — directional validation, not proof.
>
> What gives me confidence in it: the survey response rate. 52% of active users responded — that's well above the typical 25 to 35% for program-specific surveys. With 156 respondents, we have a reasonably representative sample, which reduces the self-selection bias you'd worry about if only 20% had responded. It also means the sentiment isn't just coming from the most enthusiastic users.
>
> Where NPS becomes most meaningful is when you pair it with behavioral data. The 4.2 sessions per user tells us people aren't just saying they like it — they're coming back. Sentiment plus behavior is a much stronger signal than either alone."

### Q10 [FOCUS]: "Whenever you're ready, you can begin your 2-minute memo summary."

**Mock score: 8/10 — listed numbers without explaining implications. Needs contextual and outcome-based framing.**

**What to fix:** Don't just say what you found — explain *why it matters* for each section. Connect discrepancy to renewal credibility, tickets to risk management, scalability to operational efficiency.

**Key rubric points to hit:**
- Open with the memo's objective (not the first finding)
- For each section: finding + why it matters + business implication
- End with a synthesis that ties all three back to the renewal
- Finish in 2 minutes

**Answer:**

> "This memo addresses three areas that need resolution before we can present a defensible renewal recommendation: reconciling engagement data, assessing support risk, and building a scalable analytics process.
>
> First, the data discrepancy — 340 versus 298 active users. After investigation, I'm confident this is definitional, not a data error. TechCorp counts logins and orientation attendance; our platform counts completed coaching sessions. The gap of 42 users represents people who've shown interest but haven't yet booked. This matters because leadership needs a consistent definition of value consumption to make the renewal decision — and sessions are the true measure of utilization. My recommendation is to align on a shared definition in a data dictionary so future reviews start from the same number.
>
> Second, coach response time. Eight tickets were filed, but five came from a single user — so we're looking at four unique reporters out of 298 active users, roughly 1.3%. The survey corroborates this: coach responsiveness scored 3.8 out of 5, the lowest dimension but still positive. This matters because even a few negative anecdotes can dominate an executive conversation. By quantifying the concentration and cross-referencing with the survey, I can confidently say this is localized friction, not systemic risk. That said, I'd validate with platform response-time data and investigate whether those users share the same coach before presenting.
>
> Third, scalability. With 20+ partners needing similar analyses, the current ad-hoc approach doesn't scale. I've framed BVR prep as a four-stage pipeline: automated data assembly, offshore QA with threshold-based flagging, senior analyst narrative, and templated delivery. The goal is to automate the first two stages so that senior effort concentrates entirely on judgment and storytelling — the work that actually moves the needle. I've done this before, taking a similar multi-stakeholder review from 40 hours of manual work down to under one hour by consolidating sources and automating assembly.
>
> Taken together, these three areas ensure the renewal recommendation is defensible, the operational risks are quantified, and the process is repeatable across our partner portfolio.
>
> Happy to dive deeper into any section."

### Q15: "You mentioned a >10% complaint threshold. Why 10%?"

**Answer:**

> "It's a working escalation trigger, not a scientific constant. At 10% of the active user base, dissatisfaction is likely impacting perception across teams rather than remaining isolated to a few individuals — it starts showing up in manager conversations, in team Slack channels, in the cultural narrative around the program.
>
> Below that threshold, you're typically dealing with individual experience issues — a specific coach match, a scheduling conflict, an expectations gap. Those are addressable with targeted intervention. Above it, you're looking at something structural that could affect the renewal conversation.
>
> I'd adjust the threshold based on context — a smaller partner with 50 users might warrant a lower trigger than a 500-license partner. The principle is: at what point does complaint volume shift from 'handle individually' to 'investigate systemically.'"

### Q16: "What part of this pipeline cannot be automated — and why?"

**Answer:**

> "Narrative synthesis and judgment calls. Data extraction, template population, reconciliation checks, anomaly flagging — all of that can and should be automated. But interpreting whether a metric shift reflects real risk versus normal variation, deciding how to frame a recommendation for a specific CFO, knowing which concerns to address proactively versus which to hold for questions — that requires context, stakeholder awareness, and experience.
>
> The executive story is where the analyst earns their seat at the table. If we automate that layer, we're not scaling analytics — we're producing generic reports that don't land with anyone. The whole point of automating the assembly is to free up human time for the work that actually drives renewal decisions."

### Q17: "How do you prevent metric bloat?"

**Answer:**

> "Every metric in a BVR has to pass a three-part filter: Is it actionable? Does it tie to the partner's stated goals? Is it worth the ongoing maintenance cost to track?
>
> I'd define a fixed core metric framework tied directly to renewal decisions — utilization depth, satisfaction trend, and activation growth. Those are the metrics that answer the CFO's questions. Anything beyond that needs to justify its decision relevance before being added.
>
> This is something I've learned the hard way. At a previous company, our weekly business review started clean and gradually became a junk drawer of vanity metrics — website traffic, app downloads — things that felt good to report but didn't drive any decisions. I had to push back and strip it down to what was actually actionable. The discipline isn't in what you add — it's in what you refuse to add."

### Q18: "Let's say we onboard 50 partners. What breaks first?"

**Answer:**

> "Quality control. As volume increases, small definition inconsistencies compound across partners. If each partner defines 'active user' slightly differently and the offshore team is manually adjusting for each one, you get inconsistent narratives that erode credibility at the partner level.
>
> The second thing that breaks is the senior analyst's time. If I'm personally reviewing every BVR package, the bottleneck moves from data assembly to narrative review. That's why the automation and triage framework matters — the offshore team needs to handle 80% of packages cleanly so I'm only touching the flagged ones.
>
> Standardized definitions, automated validation, and clear escalation triggers are what prevent this from breaking at scale."

### Q19: "If I gave you 30 days to implement stages 1 and 2, what does that rollout look like?"

**Answer:**

> "Weeks 1-2: Define the core metric schema — what data gets pulled, from which systems, in what format. Simultaneously, build automated data pulls from Looker and Salesforce into a standardized package per partner. This is the plumbing.
>
> Week 3: Build template auto-population and QA rules — the offshore team reviews assembled packages against predefined thresholds and flags anomalies. Document the escalation criteria so it's not judgment-dependent.
>
> Week 4: Pilot with 2-3 partners end-to-end. The offshore team assembles, QAs, and flags. I review the output, identify gaps, and refine thresholds before rolling out to the full portfolio.
>
> The key is a controlled pilot. I don't want to automate 20+ partners on day one and discover the thresholds are wrong. Build it right with a few, then scale."

## Quick Reference: Evaluation Criteria Mapping

Every answer should demonstrate at least one of these. If you're not sure what to emphasize, default to **strategic alignment** and **ownership**.

| Criterion | How to Demonstrate It |
|---|---|
| **Systems thinking** | Reference the funnel, explain why systems conflict, show how data flows connect |
| **Executive communication** | Lead with the "so what," not the data; frame everything in business terms |
| **Process design** | Describe the pipeline, phased rollout, templates, and triage frameworks |
| **Judgment** | Explain what you left off and why; distinguish signal from noise; don't over-defend |
| **Quality orientation** | Catch the definitional root cause, the 5-from-same-user detail, ceiling effects on projections |
| **Practical problem-solving** | Work with imperfect data, state assumptions, reference the Hitch WBR as proof |

## Delivery Reminders

- **Don't read.** You know the data cold. Talk like a colleague, not a presenter.
- **Pause after recommendations.** Let them react. Don't fill silence.
- **When challenged, don't backpedal.** "Great question" + direct answer + supporting evidence.
- **Show your thinking, not just conclusions.** Especially in Phase 2 — they want to see *how* you got there.
- **Own your assumptions.** "This is a working threshold" is stronger than pretending it's scientific fact.
- **End with an invitation.** "Happy to dive deeper" signals confidence and conversational readiness.
- **Hitch WBR story:** Keep to 30-60 seconds when it comes up naturally (scalability, automation questions). Problem → what you did → result.
