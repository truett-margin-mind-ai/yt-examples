What unexpected insights have you found through analysis?
Hightouch user
An issue arose where our snowflake costs doubled from one day to the next, so I dug into why. I first did a timeseries analysis and found the exact date and possible time at which the cost had doubled since our snowflake costs have very little variance day-to-day. This validated a suspicion i had that a recent fivetran bug, had increased our costs. So, I made changes which I thought would decrease the costs. But the next day, the costs where the same, so i dug in further. I broke down the timeseries cost by snowflake user. This unveiled that two users where costing us 95% of the bump in costs. One user was called hightouch, which chatting with our engineer who had set up that user, used to be used for reverse ETL, so it was an easy fix by disabling that user since it was not being used in any production capacity anymore. The other user called fivetran which was used for a whole bunch of services was a much harder task. I dug into the individual queries it was running and found that the highest costing queries were being run by intercom our support center app. So, i got in touch with our support lead and found that they were not using any of the data that snowflake was providing to intercom, so I got one of our engineers to shut off that service. The result was that our costs dropped 64% below our baseline before the costs doubled and saved us $2,400 a month.

Can you tell me about a time a stakeholder disagreed with your analysis or recommendation?

Situation: At Hitch, we had cities like College Station with highly variable demand and limited driver supply. Our COO & I proposed using "ghost trips" - where we schedule a driver to make an empty outbound trip to pick up passengers on the return leg. Heading into a supply-constrained period, our CEO was skeptical. He was concerned about two things: margins would get crushed with an always-empty outbound leg, and drivers wouldn't show up on time without passengers holding them accountable on the first leg.
Task: Based on his concerns, we decided not to use many ghost trips during that period. After it passed, I wanted to test whether his assumptions were actually true - so I ran an analysis comparing ghost trips to our alternative.
Action: I compared ghost trips against the last 5 accepted regular trips during supply-constrained periods. These "last 5" trips were the ones we had to bonus heavily just to get a driver to accept, and they rarely had return passengers anyway. I looked at two metrics: margins and delays.
Result: The data surprised everyone. Margins were essentially identical between ghost trips and those last-5 trips - because we were already paying huge bonuses and getting empty return legs anyway. But the big difference was delays: ghost trips were almost always on time, while regular last-5 trips averaged 30 minutes late. I presented this in Slack and during our Weekly Business Review. Kush came around, and we started using ghost trips more strategically in supply-constrained cities - which increased our effective supply and significantly reduced delays.

How do you decide which metrics matter most when leadership is focused on the wrong ones?
At a high level, when leadership focuses on the wrong metrics, it’s usually because they’re focused on output metrics like revenue or margins. Those are important, but they’re not always directly controllable without blunt levers like pricing.
At Hitch, we experimented with price increases to improve margins, and while margins did improve short term, our year-over-year growth dropped significantly. I used that as a starting point to shift the conversation toward input metrics—the operational levers that actually drive those outcomes.
I analyzed day-to-day performance and showed leadership that round-trip fill rate—the number of seats filled per round trip trip—was highly correlated with margins. More importantly, it was something teams could influence directly through product changes and smart, selective pricing updates. I presented this relationship clearly and reframed success around improving fill rate rather than raising prices across the board.
Over the next six months, this shift in focus helped us improve margins from roughly negative 10% to positive 20% while maintaining healthy growth, which ultimately aligned leadership around the right metrics.
